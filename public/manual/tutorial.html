Your first quest

Twenty minutes, start to finish, ending in a mod you install and play.

Twenty minutes, and you finish with a mod installed in the game. You build on top of a template rather than a blank canvas, because seeing a working quest is the fastest way to learn what the pieces are for.

Nothing here is destructive. Every step can be undone with Ctrl+Z, and the editor keeps a copy in your browser as you work. The editor says so itself, in the panel you meet first.

The screen, labelled

Open the editor with nothing loaded and you get a short welcome panel. Its own description of the job:

Drag nodes from the left onto the canvas, then wire their sockets together. Every field on the right is a dropdown or a switch — no JSON, no code.

That is the whole method, and the screen is arranged around it.

Top bar
The buttons: New, Load, Save, undo, redo, the quest tabs, Dialogues, Websites, Dry run, Addons, Settings and Export mod. Hover any of them for a longer description.
Palette
Every node the editor can make, grouped by what it does. Type in the Filter nodes box to narrow it. Drag one onto the canvas, or click it to drop one in the middle.
Canvas
Your quest map. Drag nodes around, draw wires between sockets, scroll to zoom.
Inspector
Three tabs: Node, Quest and Mod. The Node tab stays greyed out until you click a node, because there is nothing to show yet.
Status bar
Along the bottom. Whether your work is saved, how many nodes and wires the quest has, what you have selected, and how deep your undo history goes.

Start from a template

Thirteen templates come with the editor, from a blank canvas up to a 36-node quest. You want First Contact: eight nodes, marked Beginner, and deliberately no hacking in it. Its own description:

The whole spine in miniature: a brief, one objective (a person lookup), a payment and a closing line. No hacking — just the shape of a quest.
  1. Click Browse 13 templates on the welcome panel. If you have already closed that panel, use New instead — though note that New opens a different window that only clears your work.
  2. In the window titled Start from a template, choose First Contact. The editor confirms with a message reading Loaded “First Contact”.
Keep New and templates apart. New asks Start a new project? and warns that it cannot be undone, because it deletes every quest, website and conversation in the project along with the autosaved copy. It offers Save current quest first for that reason. Templates are the harmless route.

Read the map you loaded

Before changing anything, work out what this quest does. Zoom out until all eight nodes fit on screen.

Follow the blue wires. They read as "and then", so a chain of them is the story running in order. You should find a brief being sent to the player, an objective, a payment, and a closing line. That is the spine, and almost every quest you write will be this shape with more in the middle.

Now click the objective node. The inspector opens on its fields.

Why this matters. A template you cannot read is a template you cannot edit. Every quest you build later will be a variation on something you have already taken apart.

Add your own objective

Now change the quest. You will add a second objective, so the player has two things to do instead of one.

  1. In the palette, find Objective. Type objective in Filter nodes if the list is long.
  2. Drag it onto an empty part of the canvas. A new node appears.
  3. Click it. The inspector now shows its fields.

Fill in the two fields that matter for this exercise:

Identifier
A short name the game uses internally, like find-the-contact. The player never sees it, but it has to be unique within the quest.
Shown to the player
The line that appears in their quest panel. Write it as an instruction: Look up who wrote the article.

Now wire it in. Drag from the On complete socket on the first objective to the In socket on the new one. A blue wire appears, and the second objective now starts when the first one finishes.

Make it complete

An objective with nothing wired to its trigger never completes on its own. This is the step that turns a quest from a script into something that reacts to the player.

  1. Drag When event from the palette onto the canvas.
  2. Click it and open its Game event field. Pick the event that matches what the player will do.
  3. Drag from the node's When socket to the objective's Trigger socket. The wire is cyan, because it is a different kind of connection from the blue ones.
The socket has to match. A cyan wire only lands on a cyan socket. If you drop it on In instead of Trigger, nothing connects — and that is the editor refusing a connection that would not mean anything.

Give the player a reason to finish

Two nodes make a quest feel like a job rather than a checklist. Both are already in First Contact; click them to see what they hold.

A Dialogue node is a conversation on the player's phone. Select one and the inspector shows who speaks and what they say. This is where an employer explains the job and where the story closes.

A Pay the player node puts money in their account. Set the amount in dollars. A hundred is a reasonable fee for a first job; the tools the player buys run around thirty-five, so the number should mean something to them.

Check it before you ship it

The editor checks your work in two places. The status bar along the bottom tells you the shape of what you have built.

Problems show up on the nodes themselves and in the export window, not here. The fuller check is Dry run, which walks through your quest the way the exported mod would run it.

Run it and watch the order. If a node you expected never appears, it is not wired to anything. Drop a Debug probe after it and run again — the probe prints what a node does when it fires, and whether it fires at all. Checking your quest lists every message the editor can show you.

Export, install, play

Click Export mod. The window that opens shows what you are about to ship before it packs anything: your quests, your websites, the permissions your nodes need, and every file that will go in the archive.

Anything red means the game will drop or change something you built. Fix it first. Then choose Download .zip and unzip the folder into the game's mods/ directory.

Everything the archive contains and why is on the exporting and installing page. The one thing worth remembering from it: the .zip is finished output. Your project file is the thing you keep editing.

What you now know

You loaded a quest, read what it did, added to it, made one objective respond to real play, and installed the result. Four ideas carried all of it:

  • Wires decide order, not position. Moving a node changes nothing. Your quest map explains why.
  • Wires have kinds. Blue for "and then", cyan for "when". A socket takes only its own kind.
  • An objective completes when its trigger fires. That is the difference between a quest that reacts and one that assumes.
  • Exporting is not saving. Two different files, two different purposes.

Next, take apart a bigger template. The Byline adds a website clue, and Cold Call tells its story entirely in conversation. Then look at the How do I… pages when you want to build something specific.
