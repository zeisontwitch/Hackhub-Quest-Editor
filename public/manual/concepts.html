How a quest works

The mental model — eight short pieces the rest of the handbook keeps referring back to.

Eight ideas, in the order you meet them. Read this once and the rest of the handbook stops needing to explain itself.

Your quest map

A project holds three things: quests, websites, and the mod settings that give the whole thing a name. A quest is a map of nodes joined by wires, laid out on a canvas you can pan and zoom.

You build that map the way you would sketch one on paper. Drag a node in from the palette. Type into its fields in the inspector. Draw a wire from one of its sockets to a socket on another node.

Where the nodes sit changes nothing. Two nodes side by side are not neighbours to the game; only a wire makes them neighbours. The order your story runs in comes from wires, and from nothing else. The canvas invites you to forget this, because a tidy layout looks like a sequence.

Some things on the canvas are drawing aids. A Sticky note never reaches the game at all. A Story Beat is removed too, though the wires running through it are spliced together so the flow still connects. A Group frame does not run either — but its name and comment do ship, as comments near the top of the mod, so somebody debugging your quest without this editor can follow your layout. Label your frames as though a stranger will read them.

When your quest runs

Four nodes cover the entire life of a quest. They are the only ones the game starts by itself, and nothing runs until one of them does.

Quest start
Runs once, when the quest begins. Setup goes here: build the network, place the files, tell the player what has happened.
On start & reload
Runs when the quest is claimed, and again every time the game loads. Anything that must be listening goes here, because a listener set up in Quest start is gone after a reload.
On quest complete
Runs after the quest is marked complete. A Complete quest node, auto-complete, or the player's Complete button can do that.
On quest abandoned
Runs if the player gives up. Cleanup goes here.

You can use more than one Quest start node, and every one of them runs. Authors reach for that when a quest has several opening threads that do not depend on each other. Three short chains are easier to follow than one long one.

Wires have kinds

There are four kinds of wire. Each one answers a different question, which is why they look different.

WireColourThe question
ThenBlueWhat happens next in the story?
WhenCyanWhat has to happen in the game?
UnlocksAmber, dashedWhich objective comes after this one?
DataViolet, dottedWhere does this value come from?

A socket accepts only its own kind of wire. Dragging a flow wire at a condition socket will not connect. That is deliberate rather than fussy: a wire meaning "and then" arriving at something that asked "when" would be a question the game has no answer for.

Objectives are how the player knows what to do

An objective is a line in the player's quest panel. It is the only part of your quest the player is told about, so it is where the story stops being yours and starts being theirs.

An objective completes in one of two ways. Either the story flow reaches it, which ticks it off at that moment. Or an event is wired into its Trigger socket, and the objective completes when the player does that thing in the game.

The second is the one you want most of the time. It is the difference between "the story says you did it" and "you did it". Wiring a When event node to an objective's trigger is how a quest notices real play instead of assuming it.

Two details the game is strict about. Only the first event wired into an objective's trigger is used; a second one is ignored. And the amber Unlocked by socket only takes a wire from another objective — from anything else it is quietly dropped.

Set Hidden until unlocked on objectives the player should not see yet. Without it, your whole quest is visible from the first moment, spoiler and all.

Events are how your quest hears the player

An event fires when the player does something in the game. Searching for something, logging into a bank, cracking a password, downloading a file — each of those has a name, and there are 99 of them in 10 groups.

A When event node picks one of those names. When it fires, your quest hears about it and carries on down the wire. That single node is how a quest becomes reactive rather than scripted.

Some events carry information with them. Terminal.Lynx.Search tells you what the player typed, which is how a quest reacts to a specific search rather than to searching in general. The groups are named after the part of the game they come from: terminal, browser, cracking, files, e-mail, social, and the rest.

The full list is in the When event reference. You do not need to learn the names. You need to know they exist, because an unwired quest can never notice anything.

What exporting means

Your project is the editable thing: the canvas, the fields, the websites. The game cannot play a project. Exporting turns one into a mod, which is a folder the game loads.

These two are not interchangeable, and treating them as the same thing is how work gets lost. The mod is finished output. You cannot open it again and carry on building.

Everything about the file it produces, where it goes and what to do about updates is on the exporting and installing page.

Permissions, in plain words

A permission is a promise about what your mod is allowed to touch. The game shows the list to the player when they install, so it is a promise the player reads.

You never pick permissions. The editor works out which ones your nodes need, from seven in total, and asks for exactly those. Delete the node and the permission disappears with it.

PermissionIn plain words
networkThe mod puts machines, names and files into the game's world.
filesystemThe mod writes files onto the player's own machine.
shellThe mod runs commands, answers for the game's tools, or adds a command of its own.
eventsThe mod watches for what the player does, or starts another quest.
mailThe mod sends messages to the player's mail app.
bankThe mod moves money in or out of the player's account.
uiThe mod puts a message on the player's screen.

A quest that only talks to the player and pays them asks for two. One that builds a whole network to break into asks for four. Neither is wrong, but a short list is a friendlier one, and a player who sees bank wants to know why.

How quests finish

A quest finishes when something marks it complete. The clearest way is the Complete quest node, placed at the end of a success path.

You can also turn on autoComplete. Then the game closes the quest when every objective is done. Use that when the objectives alone prove success.

On quest complete is not the ending itself. It is the path that runs after one of those finish routes fires, so keep critical clues before the finish point.

Keep one deliberate finish path. A final objective, a closing message and then Complete quest is the easiest shape to read. Check it in a dry run before export.
